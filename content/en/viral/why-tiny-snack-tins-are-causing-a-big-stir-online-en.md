## The Recycling Illusion: Why "Recyclable" Doesn't Mean "Recycled"

Packaging labeled "recyclable" has become one of the most effective greenwashing tactics in consumer goods. Only 9% of plastic waste is actually recycled globally, yet snack companies continue to apply recyclable labels to packaging that cannot be processed by existing infrastructure. The Federal Trade Commission's Green Guides updates will soon require that packaging can only be labeled recyclable if it is accepted by recycling programs serving at least 60% of consumers, a standard most multilayer packaging cannot meet.

**Creighton Magid**, a partner at Dorsey & Whitney, emphasizes that the upcoming revisions to FTC Green Guides will force brands to justify their recyclability claims with actual data, not theoretical possibilities. This regulatory shift threatens business models built on misleading sustainability claims. The gap between technical recyclability and actual recycling rates creates a credibility crisis that consumers increasingly recognize. As packaging becomes more complex with multiple layers, the recycling rates plummet despite marketing claims suggesting otherwise.

Indonesia's snack packaging crisis illustrates this disconnect perfectly. Individual snack packages comprising plastic-based materials alongside organic materials, paper-based wrappers, and aluminum cups generate 34.07 kilograms of plastic waste per month. These packages may technically contain recyclable elements but are functionally impossible to separate at scale. The recycling illusion persists because consumers have no way to verify whether their packaging actually gets recycled or just sorted into landfill-bound streams.

## The Hidden Costs of Multi-Material Packaging

Multi-material packaging presents a fundamental challenge to circularity because it combines incompatible materials into a single unit. These composites are designed to provide optimal barrier properties for shelf life but create recycling nightmares. When different materials are fused together, recycling facilities cannot separate them efficiently, resulting in low recoverable content of just 20-30% in many cases. The hidden costs include increased processing time, lower-quality recycled materials, and contamination of entire recycling batches.

**Diana Maya**, of Kuraray America Inc., points out that materials like EVOH could provide sustainable alternatives by reducing food waste while enabling mono-material packaging designs. Current multilayer packaging represents a failure of imagination, where manufacturers prioritize immediate functionality rather than lifecycle thinking. The production of multilayer packaging requires specialized manufacturing equipment and materials that add complexity to already strained supply chains. This complexity translates directly to environmental costs that are never factored into consumer pricing.

The economic incentives for multilayer packaging are powerful but short-sighted. These materials extend product shelf life, reduce spoilage, and provide superior branding opportunities. However, the externalized waste costs fall on municipalities and taxpayers. When snack companies select packaging materials, they optimize for production efficiency and consumer appeal, not end-of-life recyclability. This creates a systemic failure where the companies benefiting most from packaging design bear none of the disposal costs.

## The Recycling Bottleneck: Infrastructure and Contamination Challenges

Recycling facilities struggle with composite materials that machines cannot recognize or process effectively. Multi-layer packaging often contains layers of different materials fused together, making separation nearly impossible without specialized equipment. Even when recyclable components are theoretically present, they constitute only a small percentage of the total volume, making recovery economically unviable. The bottleneck is so severe that many recycling plants cannot accept multilayer packaging, forcing it into landfills.

Contamination remains a persistent barrier to effective recycling. Even small amounts of incompatible material in recycled batches can compromise the quality of the final product. Food residue, adhesive labels, and mixed materials all contribute to contamination that reduces the value of recycled content. The infrastructure simply cannot keep pace with the complexity of modern packaging, creating a widening gap between what is theoretically recyclable and what is actually processed.

The recycling crisis is exacerbated by geographic disparities in infrastructure capability. Wealthy communities may have access to advanced sorting technologies, but most facilities cannot process multilayer materials. This creates a two-tiered system where some packaging gets recycled based on location rather than material properties. The fundamental problem is that recycling systems were designed for homogeneous materials, not the complex composites that now dominate consumer packaging.

## The Future of Snack Packaging: What Needs to Change

The shift toward mono-material packaging represents the most promising path to improving recyclability. Mono-material designs eliminate the separation challenges of multilayer composites while maintaining functionality through advanced material science. Brands like Kuraray are developing solutions like EVOH that provide barrier properties in recyclable formats. This redesign approach acknowledges that packaging must serve its purpose from creation through disposal, not just during the product's useful life.

Chemical recycling offers another potential solution by breaking down polymers to their molecular components for reuse. Depolymerization and solvent-based processes can theoretically handle multilayer materials that mechanical recycling cannot. However, these technologies remain energy-intensive and expensive, creating economic barriers to widespread adoption. The environmental benefits must outweigh the carbon costs of advanced recycling processes, a calculation that often favors simpler, more direct solutions.

Consumer demand is driving change as 70% of snack buyers now prefer brands using environmentally friendly packaging. This market pressure creates opportunities for innovation that prioritizes sustainability over convenience. The savoury snacks market growth at 5% annually through 2029 means that sustainable solutions can scale quickly if brands commit to them. The future belongs to manufacturers who design packaging for circularity from the beginning, not those who attempt to retrofit waste into recycling systems.

The regulatory landscape is also evolving toward greater accountability. **The Federal Trade Commission** is strengthening enforcement of environmental claims, meaning "recyclable" labels will require substantiation with actual recycling rates. This shift creates legal risks for brands making exaggerated sustainability claims while rewarding transparent practices. Companies that proactively redesign packaging for recyclability will gain competitive advantage as regulatory scrutiny increases.

The snack industry must urgently address its environmental impact by adopting more sustainable packaging solutions. Brands should commit to transparent recyclability practices and invest in mono-material designs.
