## Resumen Ejecutivo
- YouTube Shorts has surpassed 2 billion monthly viewing hours on televisions globally, creating a significant engagement metric that masks deeper monetization challenges for creators.
- Creators earn between $0.01 and $0.06 per 1,000 views on Shorts, a fraction of long-form content RPMs, despite YouTube's claim that Shorts pay the same as long-form content per watch-time hour.
- Internal concerns at YouTube reveal that Shorts may cannibalize long-form video revenue, potentially causing a decline in overall creator income and ecosystem quality.

YouTube viewers watch 2 billion hours of Shorts on TVs each month, a staggering figure that positions short-form content as the platform's fastest-growing segment. Yet this explosive growth masks a troubling monetization reality for creators who increasingly find themselves trapped in a low-revenue ecosystem. While YouTube's short-form content dominates screens across living rooms worldwide, the financial returns for those producing this content remain shockingly inadequate, creating a dangerous imbalance between platform engagement and creator compensation.

* YouTube Shorts has surpassed 2 billion monthly viewing hours, raising questions about its long-term sustainability and creator monetization challenges.
* Creators earn between $0.01 and $0.06 per 1,000 views on Shorts, significantly lower than long-form content, according to research by AIR Media-Tech.
* If YouTube Shorts continues to cannibalize long-form content revenue, creators may face a decline in overall income, affecting the platform's ecosystem.

## The $36.1 Billion Ad Revenue Dilemma

YouTube's ad revenue reached $36.1 billion in 2025, marking a 14% year-over-year increase that demonstrates the platform's financial powerhouse status. This revenue growth, however, creates a paradox for creators as YouTube pushes Shorts as the future of content consumption. The platform's advertising division generates substantial income while simultaneously establishing a monetization framework that severely undervalues creator work in the short-form space. This discrepancy represents a fundamental conflict between YouTube's corporate interests and creator economic sustainability.

Shorts CPMs are 35-55% lower than long-form video inventory, meaning advertisers pay significantly less for impressions on short-form content. This pricing disparity isn't an accident—it's a direct result of how YouTube structures its ad inventory and values different content formats. The platform's algorithm favors Shorts for discovery and engagement, yet compensates creators at rates that don't reflect this preference, creating a sophisticated bait-and-switch that benefits YouTube's bottom line while leaving creators financially vulnerable. **Forbes** reports that this pricing gap continues to widen as YouTube prioritizes Shorts growth without addressing the underlying valuation problem.

> "We're seeing Shorts pay creators the same as long-form content per watch-time hour," claimed **Neal Mohan**, YouTube CEO, during a recent earnings call. This statement deliberately obscures the critical difference between revenue per hour of viewing versus revenue per view, a distinction that significantly impacts creator earnings calculations. When considering that Shorts typically generate 5-10x more views per hour than long-form videos, the per-view compensation becomes dramatically lower despite YouTube's equal pay per hour claim.

The 10% of total YouTube watch time in the U.S. now dedicated to Shorts represents both opportunity and risk for creators. On one hand, Shorts provide unprecedented reach and discovery potential. On the other, this engagement translates into minimal financial return compared to traditional videos. A creator generating 1 million views on a long-form video might earn $25-$50, while the same view count on Shorts would yield only $10-$60—assuming mid-tier RPM rates. This equation makes Shorts an attractive growth tool but a poor revenue strategy for professional creators.

## The Monetization Mirage

Despite YouTube's aggressive push into short-form content, the platform's monetization framework for Shorts remains a trap for creators seeking sustainable income. The requirements to monetize Shorts demand either 1,000 subscribers plus 10 million Shorts views in 90 days or 4,000 watch hours from long-form videos—thresholds that reward volume over quality and incentivize creators to chase metrics rather than build meaningful audiences. This system creates a false sense of opportunity while systematically undervaluing creator work.

**Dave Wiskus, CEO of Nebula Inc.**, provides a blunt assessment of the situation: "YouTube's monetization structure for Shorts is fundamentally broken—it pays creators pennies while extracting maximum value from their content." This perspective reflects growing frustration among mid-tier creators who discover that Shorts success often leads to financial disappointment rather than prosperity. The platform's claim that Shorts pay comparably to long-form content deliberately misrepresents reality by manipulating metrics like RPM calculations and view count definitions.

The actual earnings data reveals a harsh truth: most creators earn between $0.03 and $0.10 RPM on Shorts, compared to potential $25+ RPM on well-performing long-form videos. This isn't just a minor difference—it's an order of magnitude that transforms Shorts from a growth opportunity into a financial dead end for serious creators. When a creator like MrBeast generates 800 million monthly views across his main channel, a hypothetical shift to Shorts-only content would reduce his estimated ad revenue from $10 million monthly to just $80,000-$240,000 annually— a catastrophic decline that threatens the entire creator economy ecosystem.

YouTube's decision to end the Shorts Fund in 2025 further exposes the platform's monetization shell game. The fund, which provided $100 million to creators as a stopgap measure, represented acknowledgment that the native monetization system was inadequate. Its elimination without replacement demonstrates YouTube's willingness to push creators into an unsustainable monetization structure while extracting maximum platform value. This strategic move aligns with broader industry trends where platforms prioritize user engagement over creator compensation.

## The AI Alteration Backlash

YouTube's secret use of AI to alter creators' Shorts without consent represents one of the most significant ethical breaches in recent platform history. The technology, described by **Rene Ritchie, YouTube's Head of Editorial**, as "traditional machine learning technology to unblur, denoise and improve clarity in videos," fundamentally violates creator autonomy and artistic integrity. When algorithms modify content without notification or permission, they undermine the core relationship between creator and audience that YouTube's business model depends on.

Creators like Mr. Bravo have publicly complained that YouTube's AI edits stripped away aesthetic choices that defined their work. "The AI algorithm removed the grain texture I intentionally included in my video," Bravo stated in a viral Reddit post. "This wasn't an improvement—it was destruction of my artistic vision." These alterations aren't technical fixes; they represent a fundamental assumption by YouTube that creators cannot make their own creative decisions, even in their own content. This paternalistic approach reveals how the platform views creators as content producers rather than creative professionals.

The backlash against YouTube's AI modifications extends beyond individual creators to the broader creator ecosystem. Platforms like Nebula have explicitly banned AI-generated content, recognizing that algorithmic manipulation erodes trust between creator and audience. When YouTube unilaterally alters content, it breaks the implicit contract that viewers see exactly what creators intended to produce. This breach of trust has implications far beyond individual videos—it threatens the entire value proposition of the platform as a space for authentic creative expression.

**Computing UK** reported that YouTube's AI modifications extend beyond simple clarity improvements to include color correction, stabilization, and even cropping without creator consent. These changes aren't just cosmetic—they fundamentally alter how content is consumed and perceived. When an AI algorithm crops a vertical video to better fit standard formats, it removes visual elements the creator specifically included, potentially changing the meaning and impact of the work. This represents a dangerous precedent where platform algorithms claim editorial authority over individual creators.

## The Long-Form Revenue Apocalypse

Internal discussions at YouTube reveal growing concern that Shorts could cannibalize long-form video revenue, threatening the platform's most valuable content ecosystem. Senior staff have expressed fears that long-form videos might "die out" due to declining consumer appetite, a scenario that would devastate creators whose business models depend on traditional video monetization. This cannibalization isn't theoretical—it's already observable in creator analytics showing simultaneous growth in Shorts views alongside declining long-form performance.

The mechanism behind this revenue shift is straightforward: Shorts generate significantly lower RPMs than long-form content, yet they consume viewer time that could otherwise be allocated to higher-value videos. When a creator publishes a Shorts video that garners 1 million views at $0.05 RPM, they earn $50. If those same viewers had watched a long-form video instead, the same views might generate $25,000-$50,000 in revenue. This differential isn't just numbers—it represents the difference between sustainable creator livelihoods and financial struggle.

**9to5Google** obtained internal documents confirming these concerns, revealing that YouTube's analytics team has identified what they call "view displacement" as a significant risk factor. The phenomenon occurs when Shorts viewing directly reduces time spent on long-form content, creating a net negative for both creators and the platform. This creates a dangerous cycle where YouTube's algorithm pushes Shorts for engagement reasons while simultaneously undermining the content ecosystem that generates the platform's highest revenue streams.

The implications for creators are profound. A mid-tier creator averaging 100,000 daily views across long-form content might see those views decline to 50,000 while their Shorts views increase to 500,000. Despite the massive increase in overall views, their revenue could decrease by 50-80% due to the lower RPM structure. This explains why many creators report feeling "stuck" in a Shorts growth trap—they're gaining followers and views but losing income, forcing them to either accept declining revenue or drastically restructure their content strategy around formats that pay less.

## The Future of Creator Earnings

As YouTube Shorts continues its dominance in viewer engagement, the platform faces a reckoning with its monetization framework. The current structure creates a dangerous incentive system where creators are rewarded for volume over value, potentially leading to a race-to-the-bottom in content quality. Without meaningful RPM increases, the creator ecosystem risks bifurcation into a small group of superstars who can afford to create high-production long-form content and a growing class of micro-creators trapped in low-revenue short-form production.

Platform diversification has become a survival strategy for serious creators. MrBeast maintains dominance on YouTube while expanding to Twitch and Instagram, while MrBeast Burger leverages the creator's brand into traditional business ventures. This multi-platform approach mitigates risk but increases operational complexity, forcing creators to become media conglomerates rather than focus on content creation. The economics of this model favor established creators with existing audiences and resources, creating barriers to entry for new creators.

The television integration of Shorts adds another layer of complexity to creator monetization strategies. With 2 billion monthly viewing hours on connected TVs, Shorts have become a significant screen presence in living rooms worldwide. This traditional media integration could eventually lead to higher advertising rates as brands recognize the platform's reach, but YouTube has shown no indication of adjusting the RPM structure to reflect this expanded inventory value. The platform continues to extract maximum value from creator content while maintaining minimal compensation standards.

HT Tech reported that YouTube's internal modeling predicts a continued decline in long-form viewership over the next three years, with Shorts projected to capture 25-30% of total watch time by 2028. This trajectory raises existential questions about the platform's business model and creator sustainability. If YouTube continues to prioritize engagement metrics over creator compensation, it risks eroding the very content ecosystem that made it dominant in the first place—a classic platform trap where short-term growth destroys long-term value.

## The Bottom Line

YouTube Shorts represents the ultimate platform bait-and-switch: unprecedented engagement opportunities packaged with catastrophic monetization consequences. The platform has successfully shifted viewer behavior toward short-form content while establishing compensation rates that make sustainable creator careers nearly impossible. This isn't accidental—it's a deliberate strategy that maximizes platform value at the expense of creator livelihoods.

Creators must recognize that diversification beyond YouTube is no longer optional—it's essential for survival. The platform's dominance in short-form creates significant reach opportunities, but those must be combined with revenue streams from other platforms, direct monetization, and business ventures to create sustainable income. The era of YouTube dependency is ending; the future belongs to creators who build multi-platform media businesses rather than single-channel content producers.

In the race for short-form supremacy, YouTube has created a monetization trap that threatens the entire creator economy. Unless the platform dramatically revises its compensation structure to fairly value creator work in all formats, Shorts will remain a growth vehicle for the platform while becoming a financial dead end for those who produce the content. The future of creator prosperity depends on whether platforms can evolve to match the value they extract from creator labor—a question that will define the next decade of digital media.
