## Resumen Ejecutivo
- Early humans, nearly 800,000 years ago in Israel, relied on driftwood as a crucial resource for sustaining fires, as revealed by a recent study from The Hebrew University of Jerusalem.
 
- The research indicates that this reliable fuel source allowed for consistent cooking and community organization, thereby enhancing survival rates (ScienceDaily).

- Understanding this aspect of early human life can reshape our view of prehistoric human adaptability and resourcefulness.

The discovery of ancient campfire sites in Israel provides a unique glimpse into the lives of early humans. Researchers from The Hebrew University of Jerusalem have uncovered evidence that nearly 800,000 years ago, these early hominins strategically settled near lakes to take advantage of driftwood, which served as a consistent and dependable fuel source. This finding not only sheds light on their survival strategies but also suggests a level of environmental awareness and resource management previously underestimated by anthropologists.

## The Surprising Role of Driftwood in Human Settlement
Early human communities were not merely nomadic wanderers; they exhibited a sophisticated understanding of their environment. The discovery at the Gesher Benot Ya'aqov site reveals that these groups relied heavily on driftwood, which they gathered from the shores of a large lake. Prof. Naama Goren-Inbar, a leading archaeologist from The Hebrew University, emphasizes that the consistent availability of driftwood was instrumental in sustaining fires for cooking, warmth, and social gatherings.

Driftwood provided a reliable supply of fuel, eliminating the need for these early humans to spend excessive time searching for wood. This practical approach to resource utilization allowed them to focus more on other aspects of daily life, such as hunting and gathering, tool-making, and the development of social structures. The study published in *Quaternary Science Reviews* highlights how the ease of access to this fuel source encouraged repeated habitation of the same site across generations, fundamentally shaping the settlement patterns of early human communities.

The implications of this discovery extend beyond mere survival tactics; they illustrate a profound understanding of resource management. The ability to plan settlements around available resources is a significant indicator of cognitive complexity among early humans. By settling in environments where driftwood was plentiful, these communities demonstrated foresight and adaptability in their strategies for survival.

## The Environmental Awareness of Ancient Humans
The evidence gathered from the Gesher Benot Ya'aqov site challenges long-held perceptions about early human behavior. Rather than being purely reactive to their environment, these ancient people displayed advanced planning and resource management. Prof. Nira Alperson-Afil, a colleague of Prof. Goren-Inbar, notes that the substantial presence of charcoal remnants in the archaeological layers indicates a sophisticated understanding of fire management that spanned tens of thousands of years.

The charcoal analysis reveals that these early humans not only utilized driftwood effectively but also understood the ecological implications of their resource consumption. The site demonstrates a deliberate approach to fire use, suggesting that early humans were capable of maintaining fires over extended periods. This ability to consistently manage fire would have provided significant advantages, from cooking and protecting against predators to fostering social cohesion around communal hearths.

The environmental awareness evidenced by these early human communities serves as a reminder of the intricate relationship between human beings and their ecosystems. The study underscores that early humans were not merely passive participants in their environments; rather, they actively shaped and influenced their surroundings, laying the groundwork for future generations.

## The Contrarian Crack: Rethinking Early Human Intelligence
Anthropological narratives have often portrayed prehistoric humans as simplistic and survival-driven. However, the discoveries at the Gesher Benot Ya'aqov site compel a reevaluation of this characterization. Prof. Ethel Allué, an expert in paleoecology from the Universitat Rovira i Virgili, asserts that the large collection of charcoal found at the site challenges previous assumptions about early human intelligence.

The nuanced interactions with their environment, as evidenced by the systematic gathering and utilization of driftwood, suggest a cognitive sophistication that has often been underestimated. The charcoal remnants indicate not only frequent fire use but also a communal approach to resource management, which would have fostered social bonds and collaborative behaviors among group members.

This reevaluation of early human intelligence also invites comparisons with contemporary societies. Just as modern humans grapple with resource management and environmental sustainability, ancient communities navigated similar challenges with a level of awareness and adaptability that speaks to their resilience. The findings at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov challenge the myth of primitive intelligence, illustrating that early humans possessed a complex understanding of their ecological niche.

## Real-World Limitations: The Fragility of Ancient Resources
While the reliance on driftwood provided clear advantages, it also highlights the vulnerabilities faced by early human settlements. The study indicates that shifts in lake levels and environmental changes could have disrupted access to driftwood, posing significant challenges for these communities. Prof. Brigitte Urban from Leuphana University stresses that such environmental fluctuations could have led to resource depletion, forcing groups to adapt or relocate.

This fragility serves as a cautionary tale about the consequences of environmental changes on human societies. The dependence on a single resource, while beneficial in the short term, could have catastrophic implications if that resource became scarce. Early human communities were not immune to the impacts of climate fluctuations, which could have led to periods of hardship and necessitated adaptability and resilience.

The vulnerability of these settlements to environmental changes provides valuable lessons for contemporary societies. As modern humans face the realities of climate change and resource scarcity, understanding the historical experiences of early communities can inform current approaches to sustainability and resource management. The study of ancient human reliance on driftwood highlights the importance of diversifying resource bases and developing adaptive strategies in the face of environmental uncertainty.
