Zone 2 training, popularized by the fitness industry, may not be the metabolic panacea it's often claimed to be. Dr. Andrew Sellars challenges the conventional wisdom by suggesting lactate is a fuel source, not a waste product.

* Dr. Andrew Sellars argues that lactate isn't a negative byproduct but a fuel source.

* Elite endurance athletes spend 70-80% of their training in Zone 2, yet its specific benefits are difficult to isolate.

* Questioning the "Zone 2 dogma" could unlock more effective training strategies for non-elite athletes.

## Iñigo San Millán's Focus on Fat Oxidation: The $106.4 Billion Question

The rise of Zone 2 training is linked to the increasing popularity of virtual fitness, fueled by promises of optimized fat oxidation and improved metabolic health. The global virtual fitness market was valued at $16.4 billion in 2022 and is projected to reach $106.4 billion by 2030.

At the forefront of the Zone 2 movement is Dr. Iñigo San Millán, coach of Tadej Pogačar and Head of Performance at UAE Team Emirates, as well as a professor at the University of Colorado School of Medicine. San Millán emphasizes controlled intensity to avoid increased glycolysis and lactate production, believing it suppresses fat oxidation.

The focus on fat oxidation often overshadows other critical aspects of fitness, such as cardiovascular health and muscular strength. Improving the body's ability to burn fat is undoubtedly beneficial. The emphasis on staying within a specific heart rate zone can be restrictive and may not always align with an individual's perceived exertion or overall training goals. Fixation on optimizing fat oxidation could neglect other crucial elements of a well-rounded fitness program.

## Ergatta's Subscription Model Drives the Acidosis "Myth"

The prevailing view of lactate as a waste product influences fitness advice and product development. Fitness companies often promote Zone 2 training as a way to minimize lactate accumulation and maximize fat burning. Ergatta, a connected fitness company acquired by TRNR, is expected to generate over $10 million in revenue in 2026, with approximately 70% from recurring subscriptions.

Dr. Andrew Sellars, co-founder of VO2 Master Inc., challenges the emphasis on minimizing lactate accumulation. Sellars cites research that presents lactate as a buffer to physiological acidosis and a valuable fuel source for working muscles.

Jim Galanes, coach at FasterSkier, suggests that there is no single, universal lactate threshold. Metrics like FTP, % of max HR, or 4 mmol points are useful heuristics but not metabolic anchors. Fixed, pre-determined training zones can be misleading, especially because people are different and have their own internal and external signals.

## Brad Stanfield's Case for Higher Intensity Training

Dr. Brad Stanfield argues that current evidence does not support Zone 2 training as the optimal intensity for improving mitochondrial or fatty acid oxidative capacity for non-elite athletes. Stanfield suggests that higher intensity training may be more effective for improving mitochondrial function and VO2max in this population.

The rationale behind Stanfield's argument lies in the principle of specificity. Elite endurance athletes have spent years developing their aerobic systems through high volumes of low-intensity training. Non-elite athletes, who have less time to dedicate to training, may benefit more from higher intensity workouts that provide a greater stimulus for adaptation in a shorter period.

The focus on Zone 2 training also ignores the potential benefits of other training intensities, such as tempo runs, interval training, and strength training. These higher intensity workouts can improve cardiovascular fitness, increase power output, and build muscle mass, all of which contribute to overall athletic performance and metabolic health. Limiting training to Zone 2 may mean missing out on the benefits of a more varied and comprehensive training program.

## The "70-80% Rule" Trap: Tour de France Fatigue and Recovery

The training regimens of elite endurance athletes, such as Tour de France cyclists, often include a significant volume of Zone 2 training, with approximately 70-80% of their total training volume spent in low-intensity zones. It's important to note that these athletes also incorporate high-intensity training, making it difficult to isolate the specific benefits of Zone 2.

Professional cyclists train for hours every day, often covering hundreds of kilometers per week. This level of training requires a significant amount of low-intensity work to build a strong aerobic base and prevent overtraining. Recreational athletes, who have less time to train and recover, may not need or benefit from such a high volume of Zone 2 training.

Over training can cause a host of problems, including hormone imbalance, injuries, and lack of motivation.

## The iFIT Paradox: Workout Surge, but Metabolic Health Questioned

In fiscal year 2021, iFIT members participated in 112 million workouts, a 229% year-over-year increase. The question remains: Does this increased engagement with fitness platforms truly translate to tangible metabolic improvements?

Many individuals overestimate their Zone 2 pace, diminishing its intended benefits. The focus on metrics like heart rate and power output can be distracting and may not always reflect an individual's actual physiological response to exercise.

A sedentary lifestyle and a poor diet can negate the benefits of even the most well-designed training program. A holistic approach to health and fitness, that includes regular exercise, a balanced diet, and adequate sleep, is essential for achieving lasting results.

## The Bottom Line

Zone 2 training is valuable, but it’s not a holy grail. Questioning the unquestioning adoption of its principles is wise. Get your lactate threshold *actually* tested, don't rely on online calculators or generalized advice.
