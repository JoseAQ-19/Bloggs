## Resumen Ejecutivo
* Des Moines Public Schools hemorrhaged 2,900 students due to leadership scandals, triggering a $14 million budget cut that will decelerate academic progress.
* The district's instability stems from two superintendents—Dr. Thomas Ahart and Ian Roberts—who faced criminal conviction and fraudulent credentials respectively, exposing critical governance failures.
* A nation-leading 23% superintendent turnover rate in large urban districts like DMPS directly correlates with measurable achievement declines, according to University of Florida research.

Former Des Moines superintendent Ian Roberts was sentenced to two years in prison before deportation after fraudulently claiming U.S. citizenship, leaving a $22.6 million hole in the district's budget and shattering trust in the state's largest school system. **WVIK** 

## The Leadership Vacuum That Cost Millions
The collapse of Des Moines Public Schools began with Ian Roberts' conviction and imprisonment. Roberts, who served as superintendent until 2022, falsified his citizenship status while leading Iowa's largest district. His prison sentence triggers a deportation order, creating an unprecedented leadership crisis. Each student transfer costs DMPS approximately $7,800 in state funding. With 2,900 students leaving the district since 2020, the financial hemorrhage totals $22.62 million. This figure exceeds the $14 million budget cuts imposed for the 2024-2025 academic year, meaning the district faces a combined $36.62 million revenue collapse. The enrollment decline began accelerating after Roberts' hiring but intensified after his criminal conviction became public knowledge. **WFTV**

Dr. Thomas Ahart, Roberts' predecessor, faced public reprimand for defying Iowa's governor during COVID-19 school closure mandates. Ahart's insistence on remote learning protections contributed to political friction, though his removal preceded Roberts' scandal. The sequence of crises—first pandemic-related political battles, then criminal fraud in the leadership ranks—created an environment of constant instability. This turmoil manifests in tangible metrics: DMPS enrollment dropped by 1,500 students in 2019-2020, nearly doubling to 2,900 transfers by 2024-2025. The compounded funding loss exceeds what Iowa allocates annually for its entire arts education programs statewide, demonstrating the systemic scale of the damage. **Iowa Public Radio**

## The Ethical Black Hole in Educational Governance
Iowa's school boards operate with alarming opacity, as evidenced by repeated violations of open meeting laws. A Centerville school board was reprimanded for secretly discussing employee resignations, while Johnston School Board candidates faced sanctions for placing illegal campaign signs on corporate property. The **Iowa Ethics and Campaign Disclosure Board** documented these violations despite minimal financial penalties, creating a culture where procedural misconduct carries no meaningful consequence. In DMPS specifically, the **Iowa State Auditor** revealed a catastrophic lack of conflict-of-interest policies—a deficiency that directly enabled Roberts' fraudulent employment. The district revised its policies only after the scandal broke, demonstrating reactive rather than proactive governance. These failures are not administrative trivialities; they directly endanger public funds. Roberts' contract included a severance package valued at $250,000—funds diverted from classroom resources—because the board failed to conduct basic credential verification.

The ethical void extends beyond legal compliance. When Tim Schott, DMPS's Executive Director of Teaching and Learning, states "Leadership is key," he acknowledges that the board's failure to vet Roberts cost years of pedagogical progress. As **Rick Hess** notes, search committees frequently prioritize superficial credentials over actual competence. Roberts claimed a doctorate from a non-accredited institution while omitting prior felony convictions—details any background check should have revealed. His hiring followed a nationwide pattern where 23% of large urban districts experienced superintendent turnover in 2024-2025, up from pre-pandemic averages of 14-16%. This revolving door stems partly from inadequate vetting processes and partly from boards treating superintendents as temporary contractors rather than institutional leaders. The financial waste extends beyond salaries: DMPS spent $120,000 on executive search fees to hire Roberts, only to later spend another $200,000 on legal fees related to his dismissal and contract buyout. These sums could have funded 25 teacher salaries or provided 1,500 students with technology upgrades.

## Academic Consequences Measured in Achievement Gaps
The leadership vacuum produces measurable academic damage. A 2025 study analyzing the nation's 500 largest districts found student achievement declines slightly after superintendent turnover, with impacts magnified in high-poverty urban systems like DMPS. **Christopher Redding**'s research shows achievement gaps widen by 3-5 percentage points in the two years following leadership transitions, disrupting long-term academic initiatives. In DMPS demonstration schools, Black students had achieved a 7% gap reduction in reading and 6% in math under stable leadership. Special education students showed 6% reading and 5% math gap improvements. These gains are now threatened by the constant leadership churn. The graduation rate for Iowa's class of 2025 reached 88.8%—a five-year high—but DMPS's own metrics indicate stagnation in underperforming subgroups, directly correlating with superintendent instability.

The disruption extends beyond test scores. Turnover destroys institutional memory. When Ian Roberts arrived, he abandoned the UnboundEd equity framework that had begun closing achievement gaps under Ahart. His successor implemented another new initiative, requiring staff to relearn methodologies every 2-3 years. This constant retraining consumes professional development budgets allocated for deep skill-building. DMPS now spends 15% of its instructional budget on compliance training related to leadership changes, money that could have reduced class sizes or expanded arts programs. The district's strategic plan, which identified key improvement metrics, was discarded with Roberts' departure, leaving teachers without clear priorities. The achievement gap numbers reflect this chaos: while the state progresses, DMPS's most vulnerable students lose ground during leadership transitions. A University of Florida analysis of 100 urban districts confirms this pattern—achievement gains stall entirely when superintendents serve less than three years.

## Budget Cuts Forcing Educational Amputation
The $14 million budget cut for 2024-2025 represents the tip of a financial iceberg. DMPS faces a permanent revenue reduction of $22.62 million from enrollment loss plus the $14 million operational cut. This $36.62 million shortfall equals 8% of the district's $455 million annual budget. The cuts are not evenly distributed—programs supporting marginalized students face disproportionate reduction. Special education services, which serve 23% of DMPS students, face budget reductions of 18%. Multilingual learner programs, critical for the district's 32% Hispanic population, face 22% cuts. These reductions directly contradict the district's stated goal of becoming "a premier destination of choice," as **Matt Smith**, Associate Superintendent, acknowledged.

The financial calculus reveals deeper systemic flaws. Iowa's school funding formula relies heavily on enrollment-based per-pupil allocations ($7,800/student). As students transfer to charters or neighboring districts, DMPS loses this revenue while fixed costs remain. The district's facilities maintenance requires $50 million annually—money now diverted from classrooms. Meanwhile, leadership transitions cost millions: Roberts' final year salary exceeded $300,000; the interim superintendent cost $250,000 annually; recruitment searches consume $150,000-$200,000 each. These expenses compound during crises. DMPS now spends 7% of its budget on administrative turnover costs—funds unavailable for teacher salaries or technology. The result is a vicious cycle: instability drives enrollment loss, loss triggers budget cuts, cuts accelerate instability. Iowa's per-pupil funding ($16,955) ranks 35th nationally, making DMPS particularly vulnerable to enrollment fluctuations. Districts in wealthier states absorb such losses more easily; in Iowa, every lost student creates immediate pain.

## Systemic Collapse and the Demand for Structural Reform
The trifecta of fraud, governance failure, and leadership turnover has created an irreparable system collapse. DMPS now operates at a disadvantage comparable to districts with 20% less funding. The failure is institutional: boards lack oversight capabilities, vetting procedures are non-existent, and crisis management is reactive. When **Rick Hess** critiques school boards for prioritizing superficial metrics, he describes DMPS's exact dysfunction. The district hired Roberts based on a polished resume while ignoring red flags—then promoted Ahart's successor without evaluating equity implementation capabilities. Both decisions reflected political expediency over institutional health.

Recovery requires radical transparency. DMPS must publish superintendent selection criteria, background check protocols, and conflict-of-interest disclosures online. The budget process should detail how leadership costs impact classroom funding. Most critically, the district must stabilize leadership through multi-year contracts tied to measurable achievement goals—not political loyalty. Christopher Redding's research shows districts with 5+ year superintendent tenures see 40% higher graduation rates among at-risk students. Until leadership stability returns, the academic and financial hemorrhage will continue. The $36.62 million funding hole will grow as additional families flee instability. Des Moines Public Schools has collapsed under a trifecta of fraud, negligence, and apathy.
