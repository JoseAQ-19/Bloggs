## Neylon's Gambit: Will Wisconsin's Crypto Staking Dreams Face Olson-Collins' Regulatory Firewall?

Wisconsin State Representative Adam Neylon has positioned himself as the leading advocate for cryptocurrency staking legalization, championing Assembly Bill 471 as the key to economic growth for the state. His legislative gambit faces significant opposition from Wisconsin Department of Financial Institutions (DFI) Secretary-designee Cheryll Olson-Collins, who maintains a strict stance on investor protection. This regulatory conflict exposes a fundamental divide between those who view crypto staking as an economic opportunity and those who see it as a securities risk demanding stringent oversight.

The bill represents Neylon's attempt to position Wisconsin as a crypto-friendly jurisdiction in a competitive landscape. With 42% of crypto holders participating in staking activities globally, Wisconsin risks missing out on significant economic activity if regulatory barriers remain. The average annual staking reward of 6.8% represents a meaningful yield in today's low-interest-rate environment, making these returns increasingly attractive to both retail and institutional investors.

Olson-Collins' position reflects traditional regulatory skepticism. "We are dedicated to safeguarding the investing public by enforcing Wisconsin's securities laws," she stated, underscoring the DFI's commitment to applying existing securities frameworks to novel digital asset activities. This approach creates an immediate conflict with Neylon's legislative objectives, as the DFI's enforcement actions against crypto firms like Coinbase demonstrate their willingness to prosecute staking activities under securities laws.

The stakes extend beyond Wisconsin's borders. With the global crypto staking platform market projected to reach $32.5 billion by 2033, regulatory clarity in major states becomes crucial for market development. Wisconsin's decision could influence neighboring states and establish precedent for how U.S. jurisdictions approach staking regulation.

Lorenzo Cruz, Vice President of Government Relations for the Wisconsin Bankers Association (WBA), has emerged as a key opponent to the staking legislation. The WBA raises concerns about consumer protection and regulatory certainty, viewing staking as a securities offering that requires traditional oversight. This institutional opposition highlights the banking sector's apprehension about competition from crypto yield opportunities.

The legislative process will likely involve extensive hearings and expert testimony from both the crypto industry and traditional finance sectors. Neylon faces the challenge of building bipartisan support while addressing legitimate concerns about consumer protection—a balancing act that will determine whether Wisconsin becomes a crypto-friendly state or remains mired in regulatory uncertainty.

## The GENIUS Act Paradox: How a Loophole Could Let Crypto Firms Steal Community Banks' Lunch, according to [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/)

The GENIUS Act, designed to provide regulatory clarity for community banks entering the digital asset space, contains a critical loophole that could inadvertently empower crypto firms to bypass interest restrictions on payment stablecoins. This paradoxical outcome threatens to undermine the very banks the legislation aims to protect, potentially reversing decades of banking sector development in Wisconsin and nationwide.

John Omahen, Vice President of Product Management at FIS, explains the intended benefits of the GENIUS Act for community banks. "The GENIUS Act opens the door for banks to offer custody services for stablecoins, and community banks could offer safe storage, extend lending against digital currency, issue their own stablecoins and even tokenize bank deposits to open the door to new customer bases," Omahen observed. The legislation represents a significant opportunity for smaller financial institutions to compete in the emerging digital asset ecosystem.

Kenneth Kelly, ABA Chair and First Independence Bank CEO, has identified a dangerous loophole that could subvert these protections. According to Kelly, the legal framework allows crypto firms to circumvent the GENIUS Act's prohibition on paying interest or yield on payment stablecoins—a restriction designed to protect traditional banking functions. This creates an unequal competitive landscape where crypto platforms can offer attractive yields while banks face regulatory constraints.

Stablecoins present a particular challenge, with an aggregate supply exceeding $280 billion integrated into payments, trading, and institutional settlement. These digital currencies increasingly function as money market substitutes, offering liquidity and yield advantages over traditional bank deposits. The regulatory disparity between crypto firms and banks threatens to accelerate deposit flight from traditional banking institutions, particularly in states like Wisconsin where regulatory clarity remains elusive.

The paradox extends to institutional adoption. Over $58 billion in capital now flows through liquid staking protocols, while an additional $19 billion has moved into restaking. Institutional investors increasingly view these yield-generating mechanisms as essential components of digital asset portfolios. Traditional banks, however, face significant regulatory hurdles in offering comparable products, creating a competitive disadvantage that could reshape the financial landscape.

Community banks in Wisconsin stand to lose the most from this regulatory disparity. Smaller institutions lack the resources to develop sophisticated digital asset products while simultaneously navigating complex regulatory requirements. The GENIUS Act, intended to level the playing field, instead risks creating an environment where crypto firms can out-compete banks on yield without equivalent regulatory oversight.

This regulatory asymmetry threatens financial stability in unexpected ways. As crypto firms offer increasingly sophisticated yield products, traditional banks may face deposit outflows and reduced profitability—potentially leading to consolidation and reduced access to banking services in rural and underserved communities. The irony is that legislation designed to help community banks may ultimately accelerate their displacement by unregulated financial technology firms.

To mitigate these risks, policymakers could consider extending regulatory oversight to crypto firms offering stablecoin-based yield products. This could involve requiring crypto platforms to adhere to similar capital reserve requirements as banks, ensuring that they have sufficient assets to cover potential losses. Additionally, regulators could establish clear guidelines for how stablecoins are backed and audited, promoting transparency and reducing the risk of systemic instability.

## Crenshaw's Confusion: Is the SEC Lost in the Crypto Staking Wilderness?

The Securities and Exchange Commission's approach to cryptocurrency staking reveals internal contradictions that undermine regulatory effectiveness and create market uncertainty. Caroline Crenshaw, SEC Commissioner, has publicly expressed confusion over the agency's guidance regarding staking classifications, highlighting the SEC's inconsistent application of securities laws to digital asset activities.

"This does not comply with either the law or the practice of the courts," Crenshaw stated regarding the SEC's recent guidance that some staking programs may not be considered securities. This internal dissent within the SEC exposes an agency struggling to apply traditional securities frameworks to novel technological architectures. The confusion extends beyond Crenshaw, with former SEC official John Reed Stark immediately criticizing the decision, saying it contradicted court rulings in cases against Binance and Coinbase.

The SEC's inconsistent approach creates significant market uncertainty. Total staked cryptocurrency value reached $120 billion in 2025 with 40% projected growth, yet these massive capital deployments occur in a regulatory gray area. Market participants lack clarity on whether staking rewards constitute securities offerings, creating compliance challenges for both established platforms and emerging startups.

The SEC's position becomes particularly problematic when evaluated against established legal precedent. Court rulings in cases like SEC v. W.J. Howey Co. established the "Howey Test" for determining whether an investment contract constitutes a security. Applying this framework to staking reveals characteristics of investment contracts—capital investment, common enterprise, expectation of profits derived from the efforts of others—that align with securities definitions.

Yet the SEC appears reluctant to consistently apply these established legal principles to digital assets. This selective enforcement creates an uneven regulatory landscape where market participants cannot predict enforcement actions based on established legal standards. The inconsistency undermines the SEC's mission to protect investors while facilitating capital formation.

The consequences extend beyond regulatory uncertainty. The SEC's confusion has ripple effects across state regulatory agencies, including Wisconsin's DFI. When federal guidance lacks clarity, state regulators struggle to develop consistent frameworks. This fragmentation creates compliance challenges for businesses operating across multiple jurisdictions and reduces regulatory efficiency.

Wisconsin's consideration of Assembly Bill 471 occurs against this backdrop of federal regulatory dysfunction. The state faces a difficult choice: either develop its own regulatory framework or wait for federal guidance that may never materialize. This uncertainty delays economic development and potentially causes Wisconsin to miss out on the growing staking market while neighboring states establish clear regulatory environments.

To address this regulatory vacuum, Wisconsin could proactively engage with the SEC to seek clarification on staking classifications. This collaborative approach could help align state and federal regulatory frameworks, promoting consistency and reducing compliance burdens for businesses operating in both jurisdictions. Additionally, Wisconsin could establish a dedicated digital asset task force to monitor federal regulatory developments and adapt its own policies accordingly.

## GSPartners' Ghost: The Hidden Dangers of Staking Pools and the Scars of Fraud

The GSPartners case serves as a cautionary tale for Wisconsin's staking ambitions, revealing the substantial risks that accompany unregulated staking activities. The Wisconsin DFI joined U.S. and Canadian securities regulators in issuing an order against GSPartners in an alleged global fraud scheme involving investments in staking pools within a proprietary metaverse. This enforcement action exposes the dangerous intersection of staking technology and fraudulent investment schemes.

The GSPartners case demonstrates how staking pools can be weaponized for fraudulent purposes. Investors were allegedly promised unrealistic returns from staking activities, only to discover that no legitimate staking operations existed. Instead, funds were diverted to support the operators' lifestyle and to create the illusion of a functional metaverse platform. This pattern of deception highlights the importance of regulatory oversight in distinguishing legitimate staking operations from fraudulent schemes.

The case reveals several red flags that investors should recognize in suspicious staking opportunities. First, promises of guaranteed or unusually high returns often indicate potential fraud. The average annual staking reward of 6.8% represents market norms, with some altcoins offering over 12%—returns that still fall within reasonable parameters for high-risk investments. Second, lack of transparency regarding validator operations, staking mechanisms, or fund custody should raise immediate concerns.

Even legitimate staking operations carry significant technical risks that retail investors may fail to appreciate. Slashing in delegated proof-of-stake systems can result in complete loss of staked tokens if validators misbehave. Centralized staking introduces custodial risks where users relinquish control over their digital assets. These technical complexities create barriers to entry that render staking inaccessible to average investors without specialized knowledge.

The GSPartners case also highlights jurisdictional challenges in combating crypto fraud. The alleged scheme operated across multiple countries, requiring coordinated enforcement actions from U.S. and Canadian regulators. This international dimension complicates investor recovery efforts and underscores the importance of strong domestic regulatory frameworks like Wisconsin's proposed legislation.

Coinbase's recent enforcement action by the Wisconsin DFI provides another example of regulatory scrutiny of staking programs. The regulatory agency issued an administrative enforcement action against Coinbase for violations of securities laws related to its staking rewards program. This action demonstrates that even established crypto firms face regulatory challenges when offering staking services in jurisdictions with active securities enforcement.

These cases collectively demonstrate the need for balanced regulatory approaches that protect investors without stifling innovation. Wisconsin's consideration of Assembly Bill 471 must address legitimate concerns about fraud and investor protection while establishing clear pathways for legitimate staking operations to operate within the state.

To enhance investor protection, Wisconsin could implement mandatory disclosure requirements for staking platforms. These disclosures could include information about the risks associated with staking, the fees charged by the platform, and the security measures in place to protect investor assets. Additionally, Wisconsin could establish a licensing regime for staking platforms, requiring them to meet certain minimum capital requirements and adhere to strict anti-money laundering (AML) standards.
